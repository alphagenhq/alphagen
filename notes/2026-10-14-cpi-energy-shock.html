September CPI: an energy shock, not a broad reacceleration

Headline inflation jumped to 3.7% on gasoline, while core held at 2.6%. The gap between the two is now the central question for the Fed on October 28.

AlphaGen ResearchOct 14, 2026
Key points
  • Headline CPI rose 0.6% in September after 0.4% in August; the 12-month rate is 3.7%.
  • Gasoline rose 9.1% on the month and accounted for over half of the headline increase.
  • Core CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year. Energy is up 20.4% year over year.
  • The FOMC meets October 27–28, with the decision on the 28th.

September's CPI report delivered a hot headline and a calm core. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the all-items index rose 0.6% on a seasonally adjusted basis, after a 0.4% gain in August, lifting the 12-month rate to 3.7%.

The driver was energy. Gasoline rose 9.1% in September and accounted for over half of the monthly increase. The energy index is now up 20.4% from a year earlier. Strip out food and energy and the picture changes: core CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% over the past year.

Why the gap matters

A 110-basis-point spread between headline and core inflation tells you this is a supply shock moving through one category, not demand running hot across the economy. That distinction drives policy. A central bank can look through an energy spike if it stays contained. It cannot ignore it if fuel costs start showing up in transportation, goods, and services prices.

Shelter is the piece to watch for that spillover. It rose 0.4% on the month and 3.2% on the year, and as the stickiest major category it is where broad pressure would show up first.

What it means for markets

The bond market's job between now and October 28 is to price how the Fed reads this report. With the 10-year Treasury yield already above 5%, a hotter headline narrows the room for error in long-duration assets. For equities, the read-through is about margins: an energy shock acts like a tax on consumers and an input-cost squeeze on companies outside the energy sector.

Our view: the core number is the one the Fed is most likely to anchor on, but headline inflation at 3.7% raises the cost of sounding relaxed. The tell on October 28 is whether the statement addresses energy pass-through directly.
